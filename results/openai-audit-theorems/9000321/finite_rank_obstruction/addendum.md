# Finite-rank local systems and a scalar-invisible finite D(2) obstruction

## Abstract

Let \(Y\) be a finite connected complex of dimension at most two, and
let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional local system over an arbitrary field.
If \(H_2(Y;V)=0\), every finitely generated subgroup of \(\pi_1(Y)\)
either acts trivially on \(V\) or maps onto \(\mathbb Z\). In particular,
a faithful such system forces local indicability. The passage from a
finitely generated subgroup to a finite source does not require finite
presentability; its generator-power construction is already present in
Howie's work.

The additional construction here is a strict separation of coefficient
ranks **on the base space**, not a priority claim for that passage.
Adjoining one generator and one relator to the verified finite-plus construction gives a finite
\(D(2)\) complex with cell counts \((1,6,7,2)\). It satisfies the scalar
tower obstruction for **every rank-one character over every field**, yet
has no finite two-dimensional homotopy model. A rank-two local system
detects the obstruction. Its image has order 24, and over \(\mathbb C\)
the representation is unitary. However, it is induced from a scalar
character on the \(t\)-parity double cover. That character already
excludes a two-dimensional model by the manuscript's original
rank-one obstruction. Thus the example does not extend the reach of
the scalar method when finite covers are allowed.
A word-derived exact certificate over
\(\mathbb F_7\) verifies the new boundary and integer abelianization
calculation. The universal topology is proved in prose, not by that
finite calculation.

Section 4.2 gives a further, presentation-theoretic application.
For both the original group and the extension, one additional
relator \(x_1^2x_2^3\) suffices to define the quotient. The resulting
balanced presentations have exact relation gap one: their kernels
need respectively five and six normal generators, whereas their
relation modules need exactly four and five module generators.
The proof bounds the deficiency of every presentation of either
group, not merely presentations related by selected rewrites.
These specific consequences are not a first finite relation gap
or a new deficiency method: BBMN Theorem A already proves exact
two-generator gaps independently of the OpenAI theorem, and its
conditional Corollary B uses the same deficiency mechanism.

## 1. Conventions and previously established inputs

For a right \(kG\)-module \(V\), where \(G=\pi_1(Y)\), use
\[
C_*(Y;V)=V\otimes_{\mathbb ZG}C_*(\widetilde Y;\mathbb Z).
\]
Vectors are rows, and matrices act on their right. Cellular chains on
infinite complexes have finite support. Rank means \(\dim_k V\), not rank
as a free group-ring module. A group is locally indicable when every
nontrivial finitely generated subgroup admits an epimorphism to
\(\mathbb Z\).

We use three results from the unchanged companion verification [V]:

1. **Finite-rank tower theorem, [V, Section 3.3].** For any field \(k\),
   finite connected \(Y\) of dimension at most two, and finite-rank
   \(V\) with \(H_2(Y;V)=0\), every map \(F\to Y\) from a finite connected
   simplicial complex with \(H_1(F;\mathbb Q)=0\) pulls \(V\) back to
   trivial monodromy.
2. **Finite plus construction, [V, Section 6].** A perfect normal
   subgroup normally generated by \(r\) elements in the fundamental
   group of a finite two-complex can be killed by \(r\) two-cells and
   \(r\) three-cells. The resulting complex satisfies \(D(2)\), with
   \(H^3(-;M)=0\) for every coefficient module \(M\), and preserves
   homology with every module from the quotient.
3. **Perfect kernel, [V, Section 4].** In
   \[
   P=\langle x_1,a_1,x_2,a_2,s\mid
   x_1a_1x_1^{-1}=a_1^4,\quad
   x_2a_2x_2^{-1}=a_2^3,\quad
   x_1=a_2^{13},\quad
   sx_2s^{-1}=a_1^5\rangle,
   \tag{1.1}
   \]
   the normal closure \(K=\langle\!\langle x_1^2,x_2^3\rangle\!\rangle_P\)
   is perfect.

These are mathematical inputs already proved and independently reviewed,
not new outputs of the present verifier. Their existing exact replay,
primary-source attributions, and judgments are retained. The field in
the first input is genuinely arbitrary: cyclic-cover chains are finite
free \(k[z,z^{-1}]\)-modules, specialization at \(z=1\) preserves the
downstairs monodromy, and a nonzero specialized maximal minor proves
injectivity upstairs. At a maximal finite tower image \(A\),
\[
d\leq d\chi(A)
 =\dim_k H_0(A;V)-\dim_k H_1(A;V)\leq d.
\]
Thus the coinvariants have dimension \(d\), forcing every monodromy
operator to be the identity. Rational Betti numbers enter only to
detect maps onto \(\mathbb Z\), not to restrict \(\operatorname{char} k\).

No asphericity, subgroup coherence, finite presentation of a subgroup,
or flatness of a coefficient module will be assumed.

## 2. Representation-relative local indicability

### 2.1 A finite source for a finitely generated subgroup

**Lemma 2.1.** Suppose \(H\leq\pi_1(Y)\) is generated by
\(h_1,\ldots,h_m\) and \(H_{\mathrm{ab}}\) is finite. There is a finite
connected presentation two-complex \(F\) and a map \(f:F\to Y\) such that
\[
f_*\pi_1(F)=H,\qquad
H_1(F;\mathbb Q)=H_2(F;\mathbb Q)=0.
\tag{2.1}
\]
No finite presentation of \(H\) is required.

**Proof.** The trivial subgroup is represented by a point. Otherwise,
choose a positive exponent \(e\) for the finite group \(H_{\mathrm{ab}}\).
Each \(h_i^e\) lies in \([H,H]\), hence is a finite product of commutators.
Lift those commutator factors to words in the free group
\(F_m=\langle y_1,\ldots,y_m\rangle\). This gives a word
\(w_i\in[F_m,F_m]\) mapping to \(h_i^e\). Set
\[
Q=\langle y_1,\ldots,y_m\mid y_i^e w_i^{-1}\ (1\leq i\leq m)\rangle.
\]
Map its generator circles to loops representing the \(h_i\). Each
specified relator is nullhomotopic in \(Y\), so choose one nullhomotopy
and extend over its two-cell. The resulting map from the presentation
complex \(F\) has fundamental-group image exactly \(H\).

The ordinary cellular boundary \(C_2(F;\mathbb Z)\to C_1(F;\mathbb Z)\)
is \(eI_m\), since every \(w_i\) is a product of commutators. Consequently
\[
H_1(F;\mathbb Z)\cong(\mathbb Z/e)^m,\qquad H_2(F;\mathbb Z)=0,
\]
which proves (2.1). Only these \(m\) relators have been chosen; they are
not asserted to normally generate the kernel of \(F_m\to H\).
In particular, \(Q\to H\) is a surjection, not an asserted isomorphism.
The dimension-preserving finite simplicial model from [V, Section 3.1]
allows application of the tower theorem. \(\square\)

The generator-power/commutator mechanism is **prior work**: see the
proof of Howie [H82, Lemma 4.1, p. 455], and its earlier use in
[H81A, p. 121]. The lemma above makes the required finite presentation
*surjecting onto* \(H\) explicit. It does not repair a supposed missing
finite-presentability hypothesis in Howie's argument.

### 2.2 The structural theorem

**Theorem 2.2.** Let \(Y\) be a finite connected CW complex of dimension
at most two, let \(k\) be any field, and let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional
right \(k\pi_1(Y)\)-module. If \(H_2(Y;V)=0\), then every finitely generated
subgroup \(H\leq\pi_1(Y)\) satisfies at least one of
\[
\rho(H)=\{I\},\qquad H\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb Z.
\tag{2.2}
\]
If \(\rho\) is faithful, \(\pi_1(Y)\) is locally indicable.

**Proof.** Suppose \(H\) has no epimorphism to \(\mathbb Z\). Since \(H\)
is finitely generated, its abelianization is a finitely generated
abelian group. A nonzero free summand would give such an epimorphism;
therefore \(H_{\mathrm{ab}}\) is finite. Apply Lemma 2.1 and the
finite-rank tower theorem to \(f:F\to Y\). The pullback monodromy is
trivial. Since \(f_*\pi_1(F)=H\), this says exactly \(\rho(H)=\{I\}\).
For a faithful representation, the first alternative can hold only
when \(H=1\), proving local indicability. Rank zero is vacuous and
causes no exception. \(\square\)

Thus every finitely generated perfect subgroup, and every finite
subgroup, acts trivially on a local system satisfying the hypothesis.
More generally, any finitely generated subgroup with finite
abelianization is killed. The statement concerns **subgroups of the
original fundamental group**, not subgroups of its representation
image. It does not assert that \(\rho(\pi_1Y)\) is locally indicable:
a finite graph has \(H_2=0\) for every representation, including
surjections of a free group onto nontrivial finite groups.

**Realization consequence.** If a finite connected complex \(X\) admits
a finite-dimensional local system with \(H_2(X;V)=0\) and a finitely
generated subgroup with finite abelianization acting nontrivially on
\(V\), then \(X\) has no finite two-dimensional homotopy model.
Indeed, transfer the representation and subgroup along a hypothetical
homotopy equivalence and apply Theorem 2.2. The subgroup need not be
finitely presented or finite.

### 2.3 Finite generation cannot be dropped

Let \(Y\) be the presentation complex
\[
\langle t,a\mid tat^{-1}a^{-2}\rangle,
\qquad
T=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\]
over \(\mathbb Q\). The matrices satisfy \(TAT^{-1}=A^2\). The evaluated
Fox boundary, in block columns \(t,a\), is
\[
D_2=(I-A^2\ \ T-I-A)
=\begin{pmatrix}
0&-2&0&-1\\
0&0&0&-1
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{2.3}
\]
Columns two and four have determinant 2, so \(H_2(Y;V)=0\).

Write \(a_n=t^{-n}at^n\). The relation gives \(a_{n+1}^2=a_n\);
the subgroups \(\langle a_n\rangle\) are nested. The displayed
representation maps \(a_n\) to translation by \(2^{-n}\), proving
that their union is isomorphic to \(\mathbb Z[1/2]\) and acts
nontrivially. Every homomorphism of this union to \(\mathbb Z\) is
zero: its value on \(a_0\) is divisible by every \(2^n\), and then
the same is true of the values on all \(a_n\).
This subgroup is not finitely generated. It is an exact boundary
example, not a counterexample to Theorem 2.2.

## 3. A D(2) complex invisible to every scalar tower test

Let \(B^\dagger\) be the presentation complex obtained from (1.1)
by adding a generator \(t\) and the relator
\[
r_5=tst^{-1}x_1^{-1}s^{-1},
\quad\text{equivalently}\quad tst^{-1}=sx_1.
\tag{3.1}
\]
Put
\[
P^\dagger=\pi_1(B^\dagger),\quad
K^\dagger=\langle\!\langle x_1^2,x_2^3\rangle\!\rangle_{P^\dagger},
\quad G^\dagger=P^\dagger/K^\dagger.
\]
The notation does not presume that \(P\to P^\dagger\) is injective.

**Theorem 3.1.** There is a finite connected complex \(X^\dagger\)
with cell counts \((1,6,7,2)\) and fundamental group \(G^\dagger\)
such that:

1. \(H_i(\widetilde X^\dagger;\mathbb Z)=0\) for \(i>2\), and
   \(H^3(X^\dagger;M)=0\) for every left \(\mathbb ZG^\dagger\)-module \(M\).
2. \(G^\dagger_{\mathrm{ab}}\cong C_3\oplus\mathbb Z^2\). The element
   \(x_1\) has order two and lies in \([G^\dagger,G^\dagger]\).
3. For every field \(k\) and every character
   \(\chi:G^\dagger\to k^\times\) with \(H_2(X^\dagger;k_\chi)=0\),
   every map \(F\to X^\dagger\) from a finite connected complex with
   \(H_1(F;\mathbb Q)=0\) pulls \(\chi\) back to the trivial character.
4. A rank-two local system over \(\mathbb F_7\), and also a finite-image
   unitary rank-two local system over \(\mathbb C\), has zero \(H_2\)
   and detects \(x_1\). Consequently \(X^\dagger\) has no finite
   two-dimensional homotopy model.

Part 3 is exactly the scalar tower test that a two-dimensional model
would have to pass. Here **all** such tests pass, not just a finite
sample or a selected character. Part 4 separates these **base-space** tests from the finite-rank
obstruction. Section 3.4 shows that a scalar test on a double cover
already detects this example.

### 3.1 The perfect kernel and the dimension condition

The image \(N\) of the accepted perfect group \(K\) in \(P^\dagger\)
is perfect. Its normal closure in \(P^\dagger\) is \(K^\dagger\):
each of the two indicated generators lies in \(N\), and \(N\) lies
in their normal closure. Since \([K^\dagger,K^\dagger]\) is normal
in \(P^\dagger\) and contains \(N=[N,N]\), it contains that normal
closure. Hence \(K^\dagger=[K^\dagger,K^\dagger]\).

Apply the accepted finite-plus theorem to \(B^\dagger\) and these
two normal generators. It adds two two-cells and two three-cells,
giving the asserted cell counts and all the assertions in part 1.
In particular, the statement is about **every** coefficient module,
not just finite-dimensional representations. For every right
\(\mathbb ZG^\dagger\)-module \(W\),
\[
H_*(B^\dagger;W)\cong H_*(X^\dagger;W).
\tag{3.2}
\]
The new argument uses neither an HNN normal-form theorem nor
injectivity of the old group into the enlarged group.

### 3.2 The rank-two detector

Let \(k\) have characteristic different from 2 and contain an element
\(\zeta\) of order three. Thus its characteristic is also different
from 3. Define
\[
S=\begin{pmatrix}\zeta&0\\0&-\zeta\end{pmatrix},
\qquad T=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
\tag{3.3}
\]
and assign
\[
x_1\mapsto-I,\quad a_1\mapsto\zeta I,\quad
x_2\mapsto\zeta^2 I,\quad a_2\mapsto-I,\quad
s\mapsto S,\quad t\mapsto T.
\tag{3.4}
\]
The first four relations hold: their scalar checks are
\(\zeta=\zeta^4\), \(-1=(-1)^3\), \(-1=(-1)^{13}\),
and \(\zeta^2=\zeta^5\). The fourth also uses that \(S\) commutes
with the scalar matrix \(\zeta^2 I\).
For the new relation, \(TST^{-1}=-S=S(-I)\).
The matrices of \(x_1^2\) and \(x_2^3\) are the identity, so (3.4)
defines a representation of \(G^\dagger\).

Order the six edges as \(x_1,a_1,x_2,a_2,s,t\). The evaluated Fox
boundary consists of the following \(2\)-by-\(2\) blocks:
\[
D^\dagger=
\begin{pmatrix}
(1-\zeta)I&-2I&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&2I&(\zeta^2-1)I&0&0\\
I&0&0&-I&0&0\\
0&-(1+\zeta)I&S&0&(1-\zeta^2)I&0\\
-S&0&0&0&T-I&I+S
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{3.5}
\]
In particular, the new row is not a scalar Fox row. Differentiating
the word \(tst^{-1}x_1^{-1}s^{-1}\) gives \(-S\) in column \(x_1\),
\(T-I\) in column \(s\), and \(I+S\) in column \(t\).
This fixes the inverse and orientation conventions explicitly.

Take the ten scalar columns belonging to the five block columns
\(x_1,a_1,x_2,s,t\). The top four block rows have zero \(t\)-column.
After simultaneously regrouping their row and column coordinates
by the two eigenlines of \(S\), their \(8\)-by-\(8\) minor is the
product of two scalar minors. Each equals
\(-4(1-\zeta^2)\); its value is independent of the entry of \(S\)
in the \(x_2\)-column. The row and column regroupings have the same
permutation sign, so introduce no sign change in their product.
The remaining diagonal block is \(I+S\). Therefore the full minor is
\[
\det D^\dagger_{x_1,a_1,x_2,s,t}
 =[-4(1-\zeta^2)]^2\det(I+S)
 =16(1-\zeta^2)^3\ne0.
\tag{3.6}
\]
Thus \(D^\dagger:k^{10}\to k^{12}\), in row-vector convention,
is injective. Since \(B^\dagger\) is two-dimensional, its twisted
\(H_2\) vanishes; (3.2) transfers this to \(X^\dagger\).
In fact the whole twisted homology vanishes: \(x_1=-I\) makes
\(H_0=0\), the Euler characteristic is zero, and (3.2) preserves
the remaining homology.

For the exact finite-field witness take \(k=\mathbb F_7\), \(\zeta=2\).
The minor in (3.6) is \(2\in\mathbb F_7\). The image of \(x_1\) is
not the identity, while the defining quotient has \(x_1^2=1\);
hence \(x_1\) has order exactly two in \(G^\dagger\).
Theorem 2.2, applied to this subgroup in any hypothetical finite
two-dimensional model, gives a contradiction.

The representation is not asserted faithful on the infinite group
\(G^\dagger\). Its image is precisely \(C_3\times D_8\), where \(D_8\)
denotes the dihedral group of order eight. Indeed, it is generated by
\(\zeta I\), \(D=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)=\zeta^{-1}S\), and \(T\).
The eight matrices \(\{\pm I,\pm D,\pm T,\pm DT\}\) form
\(\langle D,T\rangle\); its intersection with \(\langle\zeta I\rangle\)
is trivial, by their coprime orders. Over \(\mathbb C\), all these
matrices are unitary.

### 3.3 All scalar characters and the obstruction they cannot see

Abelianizing the seven relators of \(G^\dagger\) gives
\[
3a_1=0,\quad 2a_2=0,\quad x_1=13a_2,\quad
x_2=5a_1,\quad x_1=0,\quad 2x_1=0,\quad 3x_2=0.
\]
These are equivalent over \(\mathbb Z\) to
\[
x_1=0,\quad a_2=0,\quad 3a_1=0,\quad x_2=2a_1,
\tag{3.7}
\]
with no relation on \(s,t\). This proves
\(G^\dagger_{\mathrm{ab}}\cong C_3\oplus\mathbb Z^2\), not merely its
rational rank. The certificate gives integer combinations in both
directions between these two relation lattices. In particular
\(G^\dagger\) is infinite and every scalar character kills \(x_1\).

Let \(k\) now be **any** field. Every scalar character is of the form
\[
\chi(x_1)=\chi(a_2)=1,\quad
\chi(a_1)=u,\quad\chi(x_2)=u^2,\quad
\chi(s)=w,\quad\chi(t)=v,
\qquad u^3=1,\quad w,v\in k^\times.
\tag{3.8}
\]
Suppose \(u\ne1\). Then \(1+u+u^2=0\). Restrict to the original
four-relator subcomplex \(B\subset B^\dagger\). Its four scalar
boundary rows are
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
1-u&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&u^2-3&0\\
1&0&0&-13&0\\
0&-(1+u)&w&0&1-u^2
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{3.9}
\]
The first three rows lie in a two-dimensional coordinate subspace.
Thus this matrix has rank at most three in every characteristic,
and \(H_2(B;k_\chi)\ne0\).
Ordinary finite-support local chains give an injection
\[
H_2(B;k_\chi)\hookrightarrow H_2(B^\dagger;k_\chi);
\]
both complexes have dimension at most two, so no three-boundaries
are involved. This does not require \(\pi_1(B)\to\pi_1(B^\dagger)\)
to be injective. Equation (3.2) then implies
\(H_2(X^\dagger;k_\chi)\ne0\).

Consequently, a character with zero \(H_2\) must have \(u=1\).
It factors through the quotient \(G^\dagger\to\mathbb Z^2\) generated
by \(s,t\). For any finite connected \(F\) with \(H_1(F;\mathbb Q)=0\),
the group \(H_1(F;\mathbb Z)\) is finite; every homomorphism
\(\pi_1(F)\to\mathbb Z^2\) is zero. This proves part 3, for all maps
and all fields, and completes the proof of Theorem 3.1. \(\square\)

The scalar vanishing locus is not empty. More precisely, if
\(\operatorname{char} k\notin\{2,3\}\), its exact description is
\[
H_2(X^\dagger;k_\chi)=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
u=1\ \text{and}\ (w,v)\ne(1,1).
\tag{3.10}
\]
To see this, for \(u=1\) the first four boundary rows span exactly
the first four edge coordinates: the relevant determinant is nonzero
because \(6w\ne0\). The last row is
\[
(-w,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ v-1,\ 1-w).
\]
It adds a fifth independent row exactly when \((w,v)\ne(1,1)\).
Together with (3.9) and the plus isomorphism, this proves (3.10).
Thus even over \(\mathbb C\), where many acyclic scalar systems exist,
none **on the base space** supplies the obstruction that the finite-image
rank-two system supplies.

### 3.4 The detector is scalar on a double cover

The following reduction was identified by the independent Reviewer.
It is a consequence of standard induction and the cellular
induction/covering identity [HKL10, Theorem 3.1 and equation (3.1)],
not a new obstruction theorem. We give
the maps explicitly to fix the right-module conventions and to avoid
assuming that either space is aspherical.

**Proposition 3.2 (double-cover reduction).** Work over \(\mathbb C\)
with the representation \(\rho\) of (3.3)-(3.4). Let
\[
q:G^\dagger\longrightarrow C_2,\qquad
q(t)=1,\quad q(x_1)=q(a_1)=q(x_2)=q(a_2)=q(s)=0,
\qquad \Gamma=\ker q.
\tag{3.11}
\]
There is a character \(\lambda:\Gamma\to\mathbb C^\times\) such that,
as right modules,
\[
\mathbb C^2_\rho\cong
\operatorname{Ind}_{\Gamma}^{G^\dagger}\mathbb C_\lambda
:=\mathbb C_\lambda\otimes_{\mathbb C\Gamma}\mathbb C G^\dagger,
\qquad \lambda(x_1)=-1.
\tag{3.12}
\]
For the connected double cover \(X_\Gamma\to X^\dagger\),
\[
C_*(X_\Gamma;\mathbb C_\lambda)
\cong C_*(X^\dagger;\mathbb C^2_\rho).
\tag{3.13}
\]
In particular \(H_2(X_\Gamma;\mathbb C_\lambda)=0\), and the
manuscript's original rank-one obstruction on \(X_\Gamma\) already
excludes a finite two-dimensional homotopy model for \(X^\dagger\).

**Proof.** Each of the seven defining relators of \(G^\dagger\)
has even \(t\)-exponent sum, so (3.11) is a well-defined epimorphism.
All the generator matrices except \(T=\rho(t)\) are diagonal;
\(T\) interchanges the two coordinate lines. Thus the permutation
of these lines is exactly \(q\), and \(\rho(\Gamma)\) is diagonal.
Its first diagonal entry defines the multiplicative character
\(\lambda\). In particular
\[
\lambda(x_1)=-1,\qquad \lambda(s)=\zeta,\qquad
\lambda(tst^{-1})=-\zeta,\qquad \lambda(t^2)=1.
\tag{3.14}
\]
The last equality uses \(\rho(t)^2=I\), not the false assertion
that \(t^2=1\) in \(G^\dagger\).

Write \(U=\mathbb C_\lambda\otimes_{\mathbb C\Gamma}\mathbb C G^\dagger\).
The decomposition
\(\mathbb C G^\dagger=\mathbb C\Gamma\oplus\mathbb C\Gamma t\)
as a left \(\mathbb C\Gamma\)-module gives the ordered vector-space
basis \(b_0=1\otimes1,\ b_1=1\otimes t\) of \(U\).
For \(\gamma\in\Gamma\), the right actions are
\[
\begin{aligned}
b_0\gamma&=\lambda(\gamma)b_0,&
b_1\gamma&=\lambda(t\gamma t^{-1})b_1,\\
b_0t&=b_1,& b_1t&=\lambda(t^2)b_0=b_0.
\end{aligned}
\]
If \(\rho(\gamma)=\operatorname{diag}(a,b)\), then
\(T\rho(\gamma)T^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(b,a)\).
Consequently the two displayed diagonal entries are \(a,b\),
and \(t\) acts by the same swap matrix \(T\). These actions agree
with \(\rho\) on the generators \(\Gamma\cup\{t\}\).
Equivalently, with \(e_1=(1,0)\), the isomorphism is
\[
\Phi:U\longrightarrow\mathbb C^2_\rho,\qquad
c\otimes g\longmapsto c e_1\rho(g).
\]
It is balanced because \(e_1\rho(\gamma)=\lambda(\gamma)e_1\),
is right \(G^\dagger\)-equivariant, and sends \(b_0,b_1\) to the
two standard row vectors. This proves (3.12), without replacing
\(\lambda(t\gamma t^{-1})\) by a left-module convention.

Put \(C_*=C_*(\widetilde X^\dagger;\mathbb C)\), with its left
\(\mathbb C G^\dagger\)-action. Restricting that action to \(\Gamma\)
uses the same universal cover to compute chains on \(X_\Gamma\).
The required chain isomorphism is tensor associativity in the
following explicit form:
\[
\begin{split}
U\otimes_{\mathbb C G^\dagger}C_*
&\longrightarrow \mathbb C_\lambda\otimes_{\mathbb C\Gamma}C_*,
& (c\otimes g)\otimes z&\longmapsto c\otimes gz,\\
\mathbb C_\lambda\otimes_{\mathbb C\Gamma}C_*
&\longrightarrow U\otimes_{\mathbb C G^\dagger}C_*,
& c\otimes z&\longmapsto(c\otimes1)\otimes z .
\end{split}
\tag{3.15}
\]
Both maps respect the \(\Gamma\)-balancing relations, and the first
also respects the \(G^\dagger\)-balancing relation. Their composites
are the identity. Left equivariance of every cellular differential
makes them chain maps. After applying \(\Phi\), they give (3.13).
These are isomorphisms of actual cellular chain complexes, not an
application of group-homology Shapiro to a supposed free resolution.
No asphericity or tensor-exactness hypothesis is needed.

Section 3.2 now gives \(H_2(X_\Gamma;\mathbb C_\lambda)=0\)
(indeed, all its twisted homology vanishes). The element \(x_1\)
belongs to \(\Gamma\), has order exactly two, and has
\(\lambda(x_1)=-1\). A loop representing it extends to a map
\(\mathbb{RP}^2\to X_\Gamma\). Its source has zero first rational
Betti number and its pulled-back character is nontrivial. Thus this
is a scalar tower obstruction **on the cover**.

For completeness, suppose \(f:Y\to X^\dagger\) were a homotopy
equivalence with \(Y\) finite and of dimension at most two.
The subgroup \(f_*^{-1}(\Gamma)\) defines a connected double cover
\(Y_\Gamma\to Y\). It is finite and still has dimension at most
two: each cell of \(Y\) has two lifts. The map \(f\) lifts to
\(Y_\Gamma\to X_\Gamma\), which is a homotopy equivalence by lifting
a homotopy inverse and its inverse homotopies, with compatible
basepoint choices. The transferred scalar system on \(Y_\Gamma\)
has zero \(H_2\) and detects an element of order two. This
contradicts [M, Corollary `cor:torsion`], or equivalently its
rank-one tower theorem applied to the displayed Moore-complex
map. The original scalar theorem therefore suffices for this
nonrealizability conclusion. \(\square\)

The character \(\lambda\) is not the pullback of a scalar character
on \(X^\dagger\): every such character kills \(x_1\), whereas
\(\lambda(x_1)=-1\). Its conjugate also differs from it, as (3.14)
shows. Allowing scalar systems on finite covers therefore means
allowing **new** characters upstairs, not merely pulling back
the scalar systems enumerated in Section 3.3.

### 3.5 The exact surviving separation

Let \(\mathcal S(Z)\) denote the scalar tower condition on a finite
connected complex \(Z\): for every field \(k\), every character
\(\chi:\pi_1(Z)\to k^\times\) with \(H_2(Z;k_\chi)=0\), and every
map from a finite connected \(F\) with \(H_1(F;\mathbb Q)=0\),
the pulled-back character is trivial. Let \(\mathcal S_{\rm fin}(Z)\)
mean that every connected finite cover of \(Z\), including the
identity cover, satisfies \(\mathcal S\).

**Corollary 3.3.** Within finite \(D(2)\) complexes, the condition
\(\mathcal S_{\rm fin}\) is strictly stronger than \(\mathcal S\).
A finite two-dimensional homotopy model implies
\(\mathcal S_{\rm fin}\), but \(X^\dagger\) satisfies \(\mathcal S\)
and fails \(\mathcal S_{\rm fin}\).

**Proof.** The identity cover gives the implication
\(\mathcal S_{\rm fin}\Rightarrow\mathcal S\). A hypothetical
finite two-dimensional model lifts to a finite two-dimensional
model of every finite cover, as in Proposition 3.2. The accepted
arbitrary-field tower theorem applies to each of those models.
Theorem 3.1 and Proposition 3.2 give the strictness witness and the
more precise comparison:

| Test | Result for the constructed example |
|---|---|
| Every scalar tower test on \(X^\dagger\), over every field | Passes, by Section 3.3. |
| The displayed rank-two test on \(X^\dagger\) | Fails over \(\mathbb C\) and over \(\mathbb F_7\), by Section 3.2. |
| A scalar tower test on the degree-two cover \(X_\Gamma\) | Fails over \(\mathbb C\), by Proposition 3.2. |

In particular, \(\mathcal S(X^\dagger)\) holds but
\(\mathcal S(X_\Gamma)\) does not. This failure of preservation
under finite covers occurs within finite \(D(2)\) complexes:
the universal cover is unchanged, and the left
\(\mathbb ZG^\dagger\)-linear splitting \(q_2\partial_3=1\)
in the finite-plus proof remains a splitting after restriction
to \(\mathbb Z\Gamma\). Here \(q_2\) denotes the chain projection
from [V, Section 6], not the parity map (3.11). Hence the same
extension-of-cochains argument gives \(H^3(X_\Gamma;M)=0\)
for every left \(\mathbb Z\Gamma\)-module \(M\). \(\square\)

This is a non-preservation statement about the **base-space
test**, not about the \(D(2)\) condition. It also makes no claim
that all finite-rank obstructions, or even all induced
representations, reduce to scalar obstructions in the way this
particular detected involution does.

## 4. What is added, and what was already known

The strongest prior result in this campaign is the accepted arbitrary-field,
finite-rank tower theorem [V, Section 3.3], not merely the manuscript's
rank-one statement. Theorem 2.2 is its subgroup formulation using a
known finite-source mechanism. Its proof is useful for correct
quantifiers, but that reformulation alone is not offered as a
publishable originality result.

For this example's **nonrealizability**, the correct comparator is
stronger than the scalar test on the base: apply the original scalar
theorem to finite covers, then lift a hypothetical two-dimensional
model. Proposition 3.2 proves that this method already succeeds.
The rank-two detector is the pushdown of a scalar detector on the
double cover, and (3.15) is precisely the standard cellular
induction identity. There is no new realization obstruction beyond
that cover-aware scalar method in this example.

The remaining construction is Theorem 3.1 with the exact comparison
in Corollary 3.3. The original verified
example has a character detecting \(x_1\); the added relation (3.1)
forces \(x_1\) into the commutator subgroup. A scalar system can no
longer detect it, whereas the two eigenlines of \(S\) can be interchanged
by \(T\). The same relation has an invertible new-edge boundary
\(I+S\), so this change does not destroy the needed twisted injectivity.
Normal closure of the old perfect image preserves the finite-plus
input. These properties must hold simultaneously, together with the
all-field scalar classification in Section 3.3. They are verified
properties of the construction, not three new general methods.

The resulting separation is stronger than an example with nonzero
ordinary third homology, or a product already ruled out by failure of
the \(D(2)\) condition. Here the cohomological dimension condition
holds for all modules, the fundamental group is infinite, the new
involution lies in the commutator subgroup, and the scalar tower test
is satisfied universally **on the base**. The resulting homotopy type
differs from the original one: the original example has rational
first Betti number one, whereas (3.7) gives two here. Neither a
different homotopy type nor this base-space separation by itself
establishes publication-level significance.

### 4.2 Application: two balanced presentations with exact relation gap one

The preceding accepted results give a consequence beyond the
nonrealizability of the particular complexes \(X\) and \(X^\dagger\):
they determine sharp normal-generator and relation-module
generator counts. This application works for **both** groups, not
only the rank-two example.

Let \(F_n\), \(n\in\{5,6\}\), have the first \(n\) generators in the
order \(x_1,a_1,x_2,a_2,s,t\). Use
\[
\begin{aligned}
r_1&=x_1a_1x_1^{-1}a_1^{-4},&
r_2&=x_2a_2x_2^{-1}a_2^{-3},\\
r_3&=x_1a_2^{-13},&
r_4&=sx_2s^{-1}a_1^{-5},&
r_5&=tst^{-1}x_1^{-1}s^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Put \(N_n=\langle\!\langle r_1,\ldots,r_{n-1}\rangle\!\rangle_{F_n}\),
\(P_n=F_n/N_n\), and
\[
t_1=x_1^2,\quad t_2=x_2^3,\quad w=t_1t_2,\qquad
G_n=P_n/\langle\!\langle t_1,t_2\rangle\!\rangle_{P_n},\quad
R_n=\ker(F_n\to G_n).
\]
Thus \(G_5=G\) of [V], and \(G_6=G^\dagger\).
Define \(\operatorname{Def}(H)\) as the maximum of **generators
minus relators** over all finite presentations of \(H\).

**Theorem 4.1.** For \(n=5,6\),
\[
\begin{gathered}
\langle\!\langle w\rangle\!\rangle_{P_n}
 =\langle\!\langle t_1,t_2\rangle\!\rangle_{P_n},\qquad
R_n=\langle\!\langle r_1,\ldots,r_{n-1},w\rangle\!\rangle_{F_n},\\
d_{F_n}(R_n)=n,\qquad
d_{\mathbb ZG_n}(R_n/[R_n,R_n])=n-1,\qquad
\operatorname{Def}(G_n)=0.
\tag{4.1}
\end{gathered}
Here \(d_F\) is minimal normal-generator number and
\(d_{\mathbb ZG}\) is minimal left module-generator number, not
group-ring module rank. In particular
\[
\begin{split}
G_5&=\langle x_1,a_1,x_2,a_2,s\mid r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4,x_1^2x_2^3\rangle,\\
G_6&=\langle x_1,a_1,x_2,a_2,s,t\mid r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4,r_5,x_1^2x_2^3\rangle
\end{split}
\tag{4.2}
\]
are explicit balanced presentations with exact relation gap one.

**Proof.** The accepted identities give
\(t_1=a_2^{26}=[t_2,a_2]\) in both \(P_n\).
In the quotient by \(w=1\), one has
\(t_2=t_1^{-1}=a_2^{-26}\), so \([t_2,a_2]=1\) and then
\(t_1=t_2=1\). This proves the first equality; taking preimages
in \(F_n\) proves the second. More explicitly, already in \(P_n\),
\[
[w,a_2]
=t_1[t_2,a_2]t_1^{-1}[t_1,a_2]=t_1,
\]
since \(t_1=a_2^{26}\) commutes with \(a_2\). This uses no
commutation of unrelated group actions.

Use the accepted complex coefficient systems \(V_5,V_6\), of
dimensions \(d_5=1,d_6=2\). Their second boundaries on \(B_n\)
have rank \(d_n(n-1)\), and \(x_1=-I\) makes their first boundaries
onto. The cell counts \((1,n,n-1)\) therefore give
\(H_*(B_n;V_n)=H_*(X_n;V_n)=0\).
Low-degree local homology depends only on the group and the module:
the augmented universal-cover cellular chains are free and exact
at degrees zero and one, so free comparison maps and homotopies
identify \(H_0,H_1\) after tensoring. They do **not** identify \(H_2\).
The full lifting argument, which does not assume flatness or
asphericity, is Lemma 4.1 of
`research/relation_gap/theorem.md`.

Consequently, an arbitrary \(p\)-generator, \(q\)-relator
presentation complex \(Y\) of \(G_n\) has
\(H_0(Y;V_n)=H_1(Y;V_n)=0\), and
\[
d_n(1-p+q)=\dim_{\mathbb C}H_2(Y;V_n)\ge0.
\tag{4.3}
\]
Thus \(p-q\le1\). Equality would give \(H_2(Y;V_n)=0\)
while the order-two element \(x_1\) acts by \(-I\), contradicting
the accepted torsion obstruction. Hence \(p-q\le0\) for **every**
finite presentation. The balanced presentation attains zero.
An \(m\)-element normal generating set for \(R_n\) would give
an \(n\)-generator, \(m\)-relator presentation, so \(m\ge n\);
(4.2) supplies equality.

Finally \(R_n/N_n\) is perfect, so
\(R_n=N_n[R_n,R_n]\). The classes of the \(n-1\) old relators
generate \(M_n=R_n/[R_n,R_n]\) over \(\mathbb ZG_n\).
For the matching lower bound use the linear Fox relation map
\(\alpha:M_n\to(\mathbb ZG_n)^n\). Its tensor with \(V_n\)
has image containing the accepted evaluated old-relator image,
of dimension \(d_n(n-1)\). If \(M_n\) has \(k\) module generators,
then \(V_n^k\) surjects onto \(V_n\otimes_{\mathbb ZG_n}M_n\);
hence
\[
d_n(n-1)\le
\dim\operatorname{im}(1\otimes\alpha)
\le\dim(V_n\otimes_{\mathbb ZG_n}M_n)\le d_n k.
\]
Thus \(k\ge n-1\). No injectivity of \(1\otimes\alpha\)
is presumed. \(\square\)

The free-group witness is particularly small. With
\(A=t_1a_2^{-26}\), \(B=[t_2,a_2]a_2^{-26}\), and
\({}^{h}v=hvh^{-1}\), the literal free identity
\[
E=[w,a_2]t_1^{-1}={}^{t_1}B\;{}^{t_1a_2}A^{-1}
\]
has fifteen old-relator-conjugate factors. The equations
\(t_1=E^{-1}w({}^{a_2}w^{-1})\),
\(t_2=({}^{a_2}w)w^{-1}Ew\), and \(w=t_1t_2\)
give seventeen, eighteen, and two factors for the required
normal inclusions. The standalone theorem, actual word lists,
and corruption-rejecting verifier are in `research/relation_gap/`.
They are exact finite evidence, not a mechanical proof of (4.3).

| Group | Balanced relators | Normal-generator minimum | Module-generator minimum | \(\operatorname{Def}\) | Torsion detector |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Original \(G_5\) | 5 | 5 | 4 | 0 | Scalar on the base |
